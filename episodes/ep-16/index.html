Real-World Issue

  • Defining AI's Role in Education: Learning, Critical Thinking, and Public Accountability

    Artificial intelligence promises to transform education through personalized instruction, adaptive learning, and unprecedented access to information. But the educational value of these capabilities depends on how they are used. An AI system can help students investigate ideas, recognize mistakes, and develop independent reasoning, or it can become an extraordinarily efficient instrument for delivering information, demanding correct answers, and rewarding memorization. The distinction raises a question much older than artificial intelligence itself: What does it mean to be educated? In François Rabelais's 16th-century satire Gargantua, the young protagonist spends years under instructors who emphasize memorization, repetition, and unquestioning acceptance of authority. Despite acquiring considerable knowledge, he struggles to think and act independently. His education changes under Ponocrates, who integrates reading with observation, discussion, experimentation, physical activity, and practical experience. Learning becomes an active intellectual pursuit rather than a burdensome exercise in reproducing information. Rabelais's criticism is not of knowledge or discipline, but of instruction that fails to develop the learner's capacity for understanding and judgment. Nearly five centuries later, AI presents an intriguing variation on the same dilemma. The technology can supply answers almost instantaneously, but ready access to answers does not necessarily produce understanding. Used differently, it can challenge students to explain their reasoning, identify misconceptions, explore alternative solutions, and apply concepts to unfamiliar situations. It can also help teachers recognize individual learning difficulties and devote more attention to discussion, experimentation, and mentorship. A real-world example of this integrated approach comes from El Salvador, where an educational modernization initiative has combined teacher training, structured curricula, digital learning tools, AI-assisted instruction, and continuous assessment. The World Bank has described a 2026 PISA for Schools assessment of volunteer students from participating public schools as encouraging, reporting results above El Salvador's national average and comparable to the 2022 PISA averages for Germany and Sweden. The same account notes that those results are not nationally representative and do not isolate the contribution of any single component, including AI. That distinction is central to The Case of the Education Exchange. In the fictional province of El Steamador, educational Automatons work alongside trained teachers. The machines adapt exercises to individual students, identify gaps in understanding, and relieve teachers of routine administrative tasks. Teachers, in turn, use that information to encourage inquiry, discussion, experimentation, and practical application. The Automaton is one component of a broader educational system, not a replacement for it. Pistonbury adopts a very different model. Its students receive instruction directly from autonomous Automatons that monitor responses, enforce prescribed learning sequences, and emphasize repetition and correct answers. Assessment scores improve by eight percent, a result the system's proponents cite as evidence of success. Yet parents observe children who can reproduce facts and formulas but struggle to explain their meaning, apply them to unfamiliar problems, or question what they have been taught. The episode illustrates how a measurable improvement can be genuine while still presenting an incomplete, and potentially misleading, picture of educational quality. Test scores may reveal important aspects of learning, but they cannot, by themselves, establish whether students are developing curiosity, critical thinking, creativity, confidence, or independent judgment. Nor does a comparison of reported improvements establish which component of a complex educational program produced the results. Nevertheless, the contrast is instructive: Pistonbury's fictional autonomous system reports an eight-percent improvement, while El Salvador's real-world hybrid approach has been associated, in a PISA for Schools assessment of volunteer students, with results above the national average and comparable to Germany and Sweden's 2022 PISA averages. These are different measures from different settings, not directly comparable estimates of instructional effectiveness, but the contrast demonstrates how citing an "improvement" without the appropriate comparison marker can be used to misinform. The fictional contrast also highlights a broader concern about how educational technology is introduced. If AI is used primarily to reduce instructional costs or maximize easily measured outcomes, institutions may inadvertently prioritize what machines can efficiently deliver and assess over the broader purposes of education. Moreover, the apparent savings from replacing teachers may not account for the cumulative costs of maintaining autonomous AI systems, including routine calibration, software and hardware upgrades, energy consumption, and technical support. Depending on the system and its operating requirements, these expenses could diminish or even exceed anticipated long-term savings. Conversely, when thoughtfully integrated with teachers, curricula, and practical learning experiences, AI may expand opportunities for individualized attention and intellectual exploration. Who determines which approach constitutes an adequate education? In the United States, curricula, instructional methods, and school staffing are primarily matters of state and local responsibility. Federal policy influences education through funding requirements, accountability provisions, civil-rights protections, and student privacy laws. Recent federal initiatives have encouraged AI-assisted learning and emphasized the continuing role of educators, but much of this direction takes the form of guidance rather than universally enforceable instructional standards. The episode introduces a second, related question concerning public accountability. Pistonbury's educational consultant recommended the autonomous system despite knowing that a teacher-assisted alternative was available at approximately the same total cost. The fictional court's decision turns on the failure to provide decision-makers with material information about that alternative, rather than on a legal determination that autonomous AI instruction is inherently unacceptable. In real-world federally funded programs, procurement and conflict-of-interest requirements provide safeguards for the use of public money. Yet questions remain about how institutions evaluate the educational merits of competing technologies, what information advisers must disclose, and who bears responsibility when purchasing decisions shape students' learning opportunities. AI also introduces questions about the information collected during instruction, including students' answers, learning patterns, and behavioral data. The involvement of third-party technology vendors can introduce additional privacy concerns when companies gain access to sensitive information about minors, particularly if that information is retained, shared, or used for purposes beyond the original educational service. Existing privacy laws address certain uses of educational records, while the retention and reuse of derived analytics and AI model adaptations present additional governance questions. Ultimately, the issue is not whether AI should be welcomed into the classroom or kept outside it. It is whether we use AI to strengthen the process of learning, or allow the technology to redefine learning around what it can most readily measure, reproduce, and monetize. The same technology can encourage curiosity or suppress it, deepen understanding or reward imitation, expand a teacher's capabilities or displace the human relationships that make education meaningful. The choices belong not to the machines, but to the people and institutions deciding how they will be used.

What it does: This broad proposed legislation addresses AI and workforce readiness. Several of its provisions address AI adoption in education, including educator training, human oversight, student privacy, and responsible procurement. Among its education-related provisions, the bill would add AI-related in-service training to the permitted uses of funds under Title II of the Elementary and Secondary Education Act, supporting the use of AI to improve instruction without replacing educators, while retaining human oversight and decision-making authority. It would also direct the development of an educational AI risk-assessment framework addressing matters such as privacy, bias, safety, surveillance, and procedures for human oversight and override, alongside guidance and model contract clauses for procuring AI tools for education.

Potential impact: Relevance to the Episode: The bill addresses the distinction between using AI to assist teachers and using it to replace them. Its proposed procurement and risk-assessment measures also relate to the responsibilities of institutions selecting AI instructional systems. Limitation: The provisions concerning educator involvement primarily address training and guidance; they should not be interpreted as a universal legal prohibition on autonomous AI instruction.

What it does: This proposed legislation includes provisions on student privacy, parental notification and consent, and transparency and student-data protections in educational technology contracts. Existing Policy Executive direction and guidance

Executive Order 14277 was signed on April 23, 2025. The Department of Education's Dear Colleague letter is dated July 22, 2025. The Department's final supplemental priority was published on April 13, 2026 and took effect on May 13, 2026.
Latest update
May 13, 2026
Update date

What it does: Federal policy increasingly recognizes AI's potential to support instruction, personalize learning, and improve educational administration. Executive Order 14277, Advancing Artificial Intelligence Education for American Youth (signed April 23, 2025; 90 FR 17519), establishes a federal policy to promote AI literacy and proficiency, including the appropriate integration of AI into education and AI training for educators, and creates a White House Task Force on Artificial Intelligence Education to coordinate federal efforts. The U.S. Department of Education's July 22, 2025 Dear Colleague letter, a nonbinding guidance document, identified permissible uses of federal grant funds for AI-supported educational activities and stated principles for responsible use, including that AI should support educators without replacing the critical role they play, and that AI tools should be accessible, transparent, and protective of student data. On April 13, 2026, the Department of Education also published a final supplemental priority, Advancing Artificial Intelligence in Education (91 FR 18774, effective May 13, 2026), which the Secretary may use in applicable discretionary grant competitions to favor projects involving AI literacy and the appropriate use of AI in education.

Potential impact: Relevance to the Episode: These initiatives illustrate a federal approach that encourages educational AI while recognizing the importance of human educators. They provide context for the contrasting instructional models depicted in El Steamador and Pistonbury. Limitation: Executive direction, nonbinding guidance, and discretionary-grant priorities do not, by themselves, establish a general requirement that schools retain human teachers when adopting AI instruction.

What it does: Binding federal grant regulations, particularly 2 CFR 200.318 through 200.324, which the Department of Education has adopted at 2 CFR 3474.1, establish requirements concerning procurement competition, conflicts of interest, cost analysis, and financial management when contracts are supported by federal awards. These requirements can restrict participation by conflicted individuals in procurement decisions, exclude contractors that draft specifications from competing for the resulting contract, address organizational conflicts, and establish procedures intended to protect the integrity of federally funded purchasing.

Potential impact: Relevance to the Episode: The fictional dispute centers on a financially interested consultant who recommended one educational system without ensuring meaningful consideration of a comparably priced alternative. Federal procurement standards provide a real-world framework for examining such concerns. Limitation: These rules apply only to contracts supported by a federal award. Whether particular provisions apply to an outside consultant depends on that person's role and the funding arrangement. The identified regulations do not expressly establish a universal requirement to disclose every comparable instructional alternative.

Existing Policy Existing federal law and judicial precedent
Identifier
20 U.S.C. 1232a · 7906a · 7801 · 1232g · 1232h; 42 U.S.C. 2000d
Jurisdiction
United States (Federal)
Status
Existing federal law and judicial precedent
Dates
Statutes and regulations in effect. The Department of Education's Title VI regulations were amended to remove disparate-impact provisions effective July 24, 2026.
Latest update
July 24, 2026
Update date
2026-07-24

What it does: Several other legal frameworks provide relevant safeguards. Unless noted as judicial precedent, the following are binding federal statutes and regulations. Educational authority and evidence standards: Federal statutes limit federal direction or control over state and local curricula, programs of instruction, and personnel (20 U.S.C. 1232a), and bar federal officials from mandating specific instructional content or curricula, or conditioning Elementary and Secondary Education Act grants on adopting them (20 U.S.C. 7906a). The Every Student Succeeds Act also defines levels of evidence for specified educational programs and interventions (20 U.S.C. 7801(21)), although these standards do not universally govern all instructional methods. Student privacy: The Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act (FERPA) regulates access to and disclosure of protected education records. Its regulations allow a school to share records with a contractor, such as a technology vendor, as a "school official" only if the contractor performs a function the school would otherwise perform with its own employees, is under the school's direct control regarding the use and maintenance of the records, and uses the information only for the purposes of the disclosure (34 CFR 99.31 and 99.33). The Protection of Pupil Rights Amendment (PPRA) provides protections concerning certain surveys, analyses, and evaluations involving sensitive student information. Educational equity: Title VI of the Civil Rights Act prohibits discrimination based on race, color, or national origin in federally assisted programs. Since July 24, 2026, the Department of Education's Title VI regulations no longer include disparate-impact provisions (91 FR 46733). Judicial precedents provide important but distinct constitutional context: Brown v. Board of Education held that racial segregation in public schools violates the Equal Protection Clause, and San Antonio Independent School District v. Rodriguez held that a state school-finance system producing disparities among districts did not violate the Equal Protection Clause and that education is not a fundamental right under the U.S. Constitution.

Potential impact: Relevance to the Episode: These protections address aspects of educational governance, student information, and equal opportunity. They also illustrate why differences in instructional methods or resources do not automatically establish violations of federal law. Limitation: Their applicability depends on the specific circumstances. Socioeconomic status alone is not a protected classification under Title VI, and federal law does not generally prescribe a single instructional model for all schools.

Policy Gap Partial Gap
Jurisdiction
United States (Federal)
Status
Partial Gap

Potential policy gap: The fundamental purpose of education extends beyond acquiring information or achieving measurable improvements in test performance. It includes developing understanding, curiosity, critical thinking, independent judgment, and the ability to apply knowledge in unfamiliar situations. AI can support these objectives through personalized instruction, adaptive learning, and assistance to educators. It can also be deployed in ways that prioritize repetition, standardized responses, and operational efficiency. The educational consequences depend not simply on the technology itself, but on how it is designed, implemented, evaluated, and supervised. Existing federal education initiatives encourage responsible AI adoption, while established laws provide certain protections concerning federal funding conditions and student privacy. However, Judge GPT TV's research did not identify a generally applicable federal framework for evaluating the broader educational effectiveness of autonomous AI instruction or clearly allocating responsibility for its outcomes. Federal guidance on the subject is voluntary, and federal law limits federal control over instructional methods, so any such evaluation must account for the primary authority of state and local governments. Another consideration is the extensive student information that AI systems may collect and analyze. FERPA limits how a school may share identifiable student records with contractors and how those contractors may use them, but Judge GPT TV's research did not identify a federal provision that specifically addresses a vendor's retention or reuse of de-identified or aggregated student data, derived analytics, or model adaptations trained on student interactions. H.R. 10515 and S. 3063 would address parts of this area, but neither has been enacted.

Potential Policy Question

How can educational institutions determine whether AI is genuinely advancing learning, ensure appropriate human responsibility for educational outcomes, and protect student information, using existing safeguards effectively and identifying additional measures only where justified?

Policy Gap Partial Gap
Jurisdiction
United States (Federal)
Status
Partial Gap

Potential policy gap: Public education institutions are entrusted with decisions that affect students' development, communities' educational opportunities, and the use of taxpayer resources. Responsible stewardship therefore requires more than compliance with procurement procedures. It depends on informed judgment, transparency, independent evaluation, and accountability for the consequences of institutional choices. Existing federal grant regulations establish safeguards addressing procurement competition, financial conflicts of interest, cost analysis, and financial management. However, Judge GPT TV's research did not identify a federal provision that expressly requires an outside adviser to disclose a comparably priced alternative alongside the adviser's own financial interest, or that requires a recipient to document its evaluation of alternatives before adopting an adviser's recommendation. Procedural compliance therefore does not necessarily establish that decision-makers have received a complete and balanced assessment of competing educational approaches. These federal rules apply only to the portion of a contract supported by a federal award; purchases funded locally are governed primarily by state procurement and ethics law. Whether an outside adviser counts as an "agent" of the recipient under the federal conflict-of-interest rule is also not settled in the sources reviewed. The selection of an AI instructional system involves several interrelated considerations: its demonstrated educational benefits, the role of human educators, its long-term operating costs, the reliability of supporting evidence, and the financial interests of those recommending it. A lower initial price or an improvement in a particular performance metric may be meaningful without providing a complete picture of educational value. The fictional dispute illustrates the importance of these distinctions. A financially interested adviser, whose interests were disclosed, recommends an autonomous instructional model without ensuring meaningful consideration of a comparably priced teacher-assisted alternative. The underlying concern is not simply whether a procurement rule was violated, but whether the institution had sufficient information to make a responsible decision on behalf of its students and community. A related, subordinate question is whether federal policy addresses assigning human or automated instruction based on a statistical student profile. Title VI prohibits discrimination based on race, color, or national origin, but socioeconomic status is not a protected classification, and Judge GPT TV's research did not identify a federal policy specifically addressing the practice, which rests largely on state and local decisions.

Potential Policy Question

How can public education institutions make informed, transparent, and accountable decisions about AI instructional systems, ensuring that educational value, reliable evidence, long-term costs, and potential conflicts of interest are appropriately considered?
