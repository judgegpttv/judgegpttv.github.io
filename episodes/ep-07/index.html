    Real-World Issue

    • AI Agent Authority and Identity Representation

      What happens when an AI agent is authorized to act for a person—but begins saying, promising, or doing things that person never authorized? AI agents are increasingly capable of communicating, making decisions, managing accounts, and interacting with other people on a user's behalf. That raises questions that go beyond whether AI-generated content is accurate: How much authority did the human actually give the agent? Who is responsible when the agent exceeds that authority? Should the person on the other side know that they are dealing with an AI representative rather than the human directly? The issue becomes more complicated when the AI agent also uses the person's identity, voice, likeness, personality, or behavioral patterns—and continues doing so after the person attempts to withdraw consent.

    Proposed Policy

    Proposed Policy Proposed federal legislation

    The Artificial Intelligence Access, Gatekeeper Exchange, and Nondiscriminatory Transfer Act of 2026, or AI AGENT Act, would establish a federal framework for certain custodial user agents—software-based agents expressly authorized by users to interact with large online platforms on their behalf.

    Identifier
    S. 5051
    Jurisdiction
    United States (Federal)
    Status
    Proposed federal legislation
    Dates
    S.5051 was introduced in the Senate on July 21, 2026 and referred to the Senate Committee on Commerce, Science, and Transportation. It has not been enacted.
    Latest update
    July 21, 2026
    Update date
    2026-07-21

    What it does: The Artificial Intelligence Access, Gatekeeper Exchange, and Nondiscriminatory Transfer Act of 2026, or AI AGENT Act, would establish a federal framework for certain “custodial user agents”—software-based agents expressly authorized by users to interact with large online platforms on their behalf. The bill requires that this authority be transparent, documented, limited in scope, and revocable. It would establish duties for covered agents, including acting consistently with the user's directions and reasonable expectations, avoiding reasonably foreseeable harm, maintaining records of actions taken for the user, and not transferring delegated authority without specific authorization. The bill would also establish FTC registration and enforcement mechanisms and direct development of technical standards for matters including agent identity, revocable delegation, and auditable records.

    Potential impact: The proposal directly addresses a central issue raised by "Charisma Catfish": how an AI agent should operate when a person gives it authority to act on their behalf. However, the bill applies specifically to custodial user agents interacting with large online platforms. It does not establish a comprehensive federal framework governing every AI agent or every employment, dating, commercial, or interpersonal interaction.

    Proposed Policy Proposed federal legislation

    The NO FAKES Act of 2026 would establish a federal right allowing individuals and other eligible right holders to authorize the use of an individual's voice or visual likeness in a digital replica.

    Cross-Episode Episode 12
    Identifier
    S. 4591 / H.R. 8915
    Jurisdiction
    United States (Federal)
    Status
    Proposed federal legislation
    Dates
    The legislation has not been enacted. S.4591 was introduced in the Senate on May 20, 2026; on June 24, 2026, the Senate Judiciary Committee reported an amended version (advanced beyond introduction). H.R.8915 was introduced on May 20, 2026 and remains pending in the House Judiciary Committee.
    Latest update
    June 24, 2026
    Update date
    2026-06-24

    What it does: The NO FAKES Act of 2026 would establish a federal right allowing individuals and other eligible right holders to authorize the use of an individual's voice or visual likeness in a digital replica. The legislation would create civil remedies for certain unauthorized uses of digital replicas and establish rules governing licensing and other uses of those rights. The Senate version also addresses continued utilization of certain authorized digital replicas after expiration or termination of a license when that continued use remains consistent with the terms of the license.

    Potential impact: The proposal is relevant when an AI system reproduces or commercially uses someone's voice or visual likeness. But "Charisma Catfish" raises a broader issue. An AI agent may simulate a person's identity through behavior, personality, writing, decision-making, or interactive representation—not only through voice or visual likeness. The episode also raises the question of what happens when someone originally authorizes use of their identity but later attempts to end that relationship.

    Proposed Policy Proposed federal legislation

    The Senior Chatbot Protection Act of 2026 would establish transparency and consumer-protection requirements for covered AI chatbots.

    Identifier
    S. 5117
    Jurisdiction
    United States (Federal)
    Status
    Proposed federal legislation
    Dates
    S.5117 was introduced on July 23, 2026 and referred to the Senate Committee on Commerce, Science, and Transportation. It has not been enacted.
    Latest update
    July 23, 2026
    Update date
    2026-07-23

    What it does: The Senior Chatbot Protection Act of 2026 would establish transparency and consumer-protection requirements for covered AI chatbots. Among its provisions, the bill would require chatbots to disclose that they are artificial intelligence and not human and would prohibit specified false representations that a chatbot is human or a licensed professional. It also includes additional requirements involving human oversight, high-stakes decisions, crisis situations, and certain interactions involving older users.

    Potential impact: The bill directly addresses an important part of the Episode 7 issue: whether people interacting with AI should know that they are dealing with AI rather than a human. It does not, however, establish a general rule requiring an AI agent to disclose that it is acting on behalf of a particular private individual. Knowing “this is AI” is not necessarily the same as knowing “this is an AI representative speaking for this person.”

    The AI Impersonation Prevention Act of 2025 would amend existing federal criminal law concerning impersonation of federal officers and employees.

    Identifier
    H.R. 4628
    Jurisdiction
    United States (Federal)
    Status
    Proposed federal legislation
    Dates
    H.R.4628 was introduced in the House on July 23, 2025 and referred to the House Committee on the Judiciary. It has not been enacted.
    Latest update
    July 23, 2025
    Update date
    2025-07-23

    What it does: The AI Impersonation Prevention Act of 2025 would amend existing federal criminal law concerning impersonation of federal officers and employees. The proposal would address knowing use of artificial intelligence to impersonate or falsely represent a federal officer or employee, including certain uses of a federal official's voice or likeness without an explicit disclaimer.

    Potential impact: The bill demonstrates one approach Congress has proposed for AI-enabled impersonation and disclosure. Its scope is narrow, however: it concerns impersonation of federal officers and employees, not AI agents representing private individuals in employment, dating, or ordinary commercial interactions. It therefore provides limited context for Episode 7 rather than directly regulating the BellaPrime scenario.

    The Protecting Consumers from Deceptive AI Act would direct NIST to establish task forces supporting technical standards and guidelines for identifying generative-AI content and require disclosure for covered generative-AI audio or visual content.

    Cross-Episode Episode 14

    Jurisdiction
    United States (Federal)
    Status
    Proposed federal legislation
    Dates

    Latest update

    What it does: The Protecting Consumers from Deceptive AI Act would direct the National Institute of Standards and Technology to establish task forces supporting technical standards and guidelines for identifying generative-AI content. Episode 7 highlights that distinction: identifying content as AI-generated does not necessarily tell an employer, customer, potential date, or other person that an AI system is communicating or making representations on someone's behalf.

    Existing Policy Existing federal law

    Section 5 of the Federal Trade Commission Act prohibits unfair or deceptive acts or practices in or affecting commerce.

    Cross-Episode Episode 5 · Episode 14
    Identifier
    FTC Act § 5
    Jurisdiction
    United States (Federal)
    Status
    Existing federal law
    Dates
    Enacted September 26, 1914 (15 U.S.C. § 45). In effect.
    Latest update
    September 26, 1914
    Update date
    1914-09-26

    What it does: Section 5 of the Federal Trade Commission Act prohibits unfair or deceptive acts or practices in or affecting commerce. A deceptive practice can involve a material representation, omission, or practice likely to mislead a consumer acting reasonably under the circumstances.

    Potential impact: Section 5 provides an existing federal framework that may apply when companies make deceptive representations about AI products or services or engage in unfair or deceptive commercial practices involving AI. It does not, however, create a comprehensive framework governing how much authority a person may delegate to an AI agent, when an AI agent's representations legally bind that person, or how responsibility should be allocated when an AI agent exceeds its authorized role.

    Existing Policy Existing federal law

    The Electronic Signatures in Global and National Commerce Act, or E-SIGN Act, already recognizes the concept of an electronic agent.

    Identifier
    E-SIGN Act · Public Law 106-229
    Jurisdiction
    United States (Federal)
    Status
    Existing federal law
    Dates
    Became Public Law No. 106-229 on June 30, 2000. Main title provisions generally effective October 1, 2000. In effect.
    Latest update
    June 30, 2000
    Update date
    2000-06-30

    What it does: The Electronic Signatures in Global and National Commerce Act, or E-SIGN Act, already recognizes the concept of an “electronic agent.” The law defines an electronic agent broadly as a computer program or other automated means used independently to initiate an action or respond to electronic records or transactions without an individual reviewing or acting at the time of that particular action or response.

    Potential impact: The E-SIGN Act demonstrates that federal law recognized automated electronic agents well before today's generation of autonomous AI systems. But the statute does not establish a comprehensive framework for modern AI-agent issues such as delegated authority, representation disclosure, misrepresentation, or allocation of responsibility when an autonomous agent exceeds a person's instructions.

    Policy Gap

    Policy Gap Partial Gap
    Jurisdiction
    United States (Federal)
    Status
    Partial Gap

    Potential policy gap: Existing federal law already addresses aspects of automated transactions and deceptive commercial conduct, while proposed legislation has begun addressing AI-agent delegation, chatbot disclosure, AI-generated-content labeling, and particular forms of impersonation. But these frameworks do not comprehensively answer the combination of questions raised when an AI agent acts for a particular person: What was the agent authorized to do? Who is responsible if it exceeds that authority? And does the person interacting with the agent know that it is an AI acting for someone else? The distinction is important. An AI system may truthfully disclose that it is artificial intelligence without clearly explaining whose interests it represents or what authority that person has given it.

    Potential Policy Question

    As AI agents increasingly act on behalf of individuals, should federal policy more clearly define their delegated authority, responsibility for actions outside that authority, and what they must disclose to the people with whom they interact?

    Policy Gap Partial Gap
    Jurisdiction
    United States (Federal)
    Status
    Partial Gap

    Potential policy gap: Proposed federal legislation such as the NO FAKES Act would establish rights concerning unauthorized digital replicas of a person's voice or visual likeness. But an autonomous AI representation can involve more than voice or appearance. It may reproduce or simulate a person's communication patterns, behavior, personality, preferences, or decision-making while continuing to interact with other people as that person's representative. That creates a broader policy question when a person originally authorizes an AI service to use elements of their identity but later withdraws from the relationship. The NO FAKES Act addresses certain licensed digital replicas, but it does not comprehensively resolve continued commercial operation of an autonomous AI identity extending beyond voice and visual likeness.

    Potential Policy Question

    When a person withdraws consent from an AI service, what rights should govern the continued commercial use of an AI agent that was trained or authorized to represent that person's identity?
