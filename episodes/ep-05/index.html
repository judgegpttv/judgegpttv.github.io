Real-World Issue

  • Ownership of AI-Enabled Devices and Learned Personalization

    When you own an AI-enabled device, what exactly do you own—and what happens when more than one person helped shape it? An ordinary consumer product can usually be transferred with the object itself. An AI-enabled companion may be more complicated. The physical device can contain software, user-generated data, cloud-based services, and learned personalization developed through months or years of interaction. Multiple people may contribute preferences, information, instructions, recordings, or other inputs that influence how the system behaves. If those users later disagree over who should keep or control the device, ownership of the physical product may answer only part of the question. Federal law addresses some of these layers separately. But emerging AI-enabled products raise a broader question: when the device changes hands, what happens to the digital history and learned personalization inside it?

Proposed Policy Proposed federal legislation

The You Own the Data Act, or YODA, would establish that covered data is the property of the user as the user creates it.

Identifier
H.R. 8652
Jurisdiction
United States (Federal)
Status
Proposed federal legislation
Dates
Introduced in the House of Representatives on May 4, 2026, and referred to the House Committee on Energy and Commerce. It has not been enacted.
Latest update
May 4, 2026
Update date
2026-05-04

What it does: The You Own the Data Act, or YODA, would establish that covered data is the property of the user as the user creates it. The proposal would also establish rights involving access, correction, deletion, portability, and transfer of covered data and would restrict certain requirements to transfer or monetize private data in exchange for services.

Potential impact: For an AI-enabled companion, YODA could provide a clearer federal principle for one important part of the product: the user's data. But an AI device can contain more than user data. YODA does not establish ownership rules for the physical device, underlying software, learned behavior or personalization, cloud or account rights, or conflicts between multiple users who contributed data to the same system. It therefore addresses one layer of the Episode 5 ownership question rather than the entire product.

Proposed Policy Proposed federal legislation

The Online Privacy Act of 2026 proposes federal rights concerning personal data, including access, correction, deletion and portability, along with additional privacy protections.

Identifier
H.R. 8014
Jurisdiction
United States (Federal)
Status
Proposed federal legislation
Dates
Introduced in the House of Representatives on March 19, 2026. It has not been enacted.
Latest update
March 19, 2026
Update date
2026-03-19

What it does: The Online Privacy Act of 2026 proposes federal rights concerning personal data, including access, correction, deletion and portability, along with additional privacy protections. It would also establish a federal Digital Privacy Agency.

Potential impact: Those rights could affect what happens to personal data accumulated through an AI-enabled consumer product, particularly when a user wants to retrieve, transfer, correct, or delete information associated with the service. Like YODA, however, the proposal does not establish a comprehensive framework for ownership of the entire AI-enabled product. It does not determine ownership of the physical device, software or learned personalization, nor does it establish a system for resolving control disputes between multiple users.

Existing Policy Existing federal law / agency guidance

Federal copyright law can protect software and qualifying human-authored expression associated with an AI-enabled product.

Cross-Episode Episode 9
Identifier
U.S. Copyright Law · Copyright Office AI Reports
Jurisdiction
United States (Federal)
Status
Existing federal law / agency guidance
Dates
Federal copyright law remains in effect. The U.S. Copyright Office Part 2 (Copyrightability) report was issued January 29, 2025.
Latest update
January 29, 2025
Update date
2025-01-29

What it does: Federal copyright law can protect software and qualifying human-authored expression associated with an AI-enabled product. The U.S. Copyright Office has also examined how existing copyright principles apply to material created with generative AI. Its 2025 report on copyrightability explains that AI-assisted works may receive copyright protection when sufficient human-authored expression is present, while purely AI-generated material does not receive copyright protection merely because a person prompted the system.

Potential impact: Copyright law helps distinguish between the physical device and some of the intellectual property associated with it. Owning an AI-enabled product does not necessarily mean owning the copyright in its software or every protected work incorporated into the system. The situation becomes less clear when considering learned behavior and personalization developed through interaction with users. The Copyright Office's AI copyrightability report does not specifically determine ownership of AI-learned behavior, personalization models, or behavioral data generated through user interactions. Existing copyright principles therefore provide part of the legal framework without resolving the broader ownership question raised by increasingly personalized AI devices.

Sources

  • U.S. Copyright Office — Copyright and Artificial Intelligence
  • U.S. Copyright Office — Copyright and Artificial Intelligence, Part 2: Copyrightability

Existing Policy Existing federal law

Section 5 of the Federal Trade Commission Act prohibits unfair or deceptive acts or practices in or affecting commerce.

Cross-Episode Episode 7 · Episode 14
Identifier
FTC Act § 5
Jurisdiction
United States (Federal)
Status
Existing federal law
Dates
Enacted September 26, 1914
Latest update
September 26, 1914
Update date
1914-09-26

What it does: Section 5 of the Federal Trade Commission Act prohibits unfair or deceptive acts or practices in or affecting commerce. That authority can apply to representations companies make about consumer products and services, including practices involving privacy, data handling and AI-enabled services.

Potential impact: For AI-enabled companion products, Section 5 can provide a federal consumer-protection backstop when a provider's representations or commercial practices involving consumer data are unfair or deceptive. But Section 5 is not an ownership framework. It does not determine who owns an AI-enabled device, its software, accumulated user data, learned personalization, or cloud account, and it does not establish rules for dividing control between multiple authorized users.

Potential policy gap: Existing law addresses different parts of an AI-enabled product separately—including the physical device, software, copyrighted human contributions, consumer data, and contractual account rights. But Judge GPT TV's research did not identify a comprehensive federal framework defining what happens to those different interests when an AI-enabled device is sold, transferred, divided, or disputed between users. This creates an emerging distinction between owning the device and owning or controlling everything that makes the device personalized to its users.

Potential Policy Question

When ownership of an AI-enabled device changes, what rights should transfer with the physical product—and what rights should remain with its users, software provider, or other rights holders?

Policy Gap Partial Gap
Jurisdiction
United States (Federal)
Status
Partial Gap

Potential policy gap: AI-enabled devices can develop personalized behavior through interactions with multiple users. Those users may contribute data, preferences, instructions and other information over time, while access and control may also depend on accounts, software licenses, product design and provider terms. Problems can emerge when authorized users later disagree. One person may want to retain the device, another may want their information removed, and both may claim an interest in the personalization they helped create. The same control problem can extend to device capabilities such as recording, tracking or monitoring when one authorized user activates those functions in ways another user opposes. Judge GPT TV's research did not identify a comprehensive federal framework defining how control, accumulated personalization, or user-generated data should be handled when multiple authorized users of an AI-enabled consumer product later disagree.

Potential Policy Question

When multiple people contribute data, preferences, customization, or personalization to the same AI-enabled device, how should control of that shared digital history be handled if the users later disagree?
