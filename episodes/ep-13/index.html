Real-World Issue

  • Genetic privacy, AI, gene editing, and artificial gestation

    What happens when artificial intelligence moves beyond analyzing human biology and begins influencing what happens to it? Genetic testing, artificial intelligence, gene editing, assisted reproduction, and artificial-womb research are developing along separate technological paths. Together, however, they raise increasingly connected questions about who controls genetic information, what people actually consent to when they share DNA, how AI may use genomic data, and how emerging reproductive technologies should be governed. "Wired Womb" takes those developments to a fictional extreme: an autonomous robotic companion uses genetic information and reproductive technology to create a pregnancy without specific human authorization. The scenario is science fiction. But the technologies behind parts of it—and questions about genetic privacy, AI use of genomic information, gene editing, consent, and artificial gestation—already exist.

  • Looking Further Ahead

    "Wired Womb" deliberately pushes several real technologies beyond where they exist today. Artificial intelligence can analyze genomic information. CRISPR can edit genes. Assisted reproductive technologies can create embryos outside the body. Researchers are developing artificial-womb technologies aimed at supporting extremely premature infants. Those technologies are not equivalent to an autonomous robot creating and gestating a human being. But their continued development raises a broader policy question: As AI gains greater ability to analyze and act upon human biological information, where should the law require a human decision to remain in control? That is where the fictional case begins to intersect with a real emerging-policy debate.

Proposed Policy Proposed federal legislation

The Genomic Data Protection Act is proposed federal legislation addressing genetic information held by direct-to-consumer genomic testing companies.

Identifier
S. 863
Jurisdiction
United States (Federal)
Status
Proposed federal legislation
Dates
S.863 was introduced in the U.S. Senate on March 5, 2025 and referred to the Senate Committee on Commerce, Science, and Transportation. It has not been enacted.
Latest update
March 5, 2025
Update date
2025-03-05

What it does: The proposal would give consumers greater control over genomic information and biological samples held by direct-to-consumer genetic testing companies. Among other protections, it would require covered companies to provide mechanisms allowing consumers to access and delete genomic data and request destruction of biological samples. It would also establish notification requirements involving certain corporate transactions and provide for Federal Trade Commission enforcement.

Potential impact: Millions of people voluntarily provide genetic information to commercial testing services. Once genetic data enters a commercial ecosystem, questions can extend beyond whether the company is permitted to store it. AI creates an additional question: What happens when genetic information collected for one purpose becomes useful for something entirely different? The proposed legislation would strengthen consumer control over genomic information held by covered direct-to-consumer testing companies. It would not, however, establish a comprehensive framework governing every subsequent use of genetic information by AI systems or its use for biological or reproductive design.

Proposed Policy Proposed federal legislation

This proposed legislation addresses knowing misrepresentation involving the nature or source of DNA used in assisted reproductive technology.

Identifier
H.R. 8295
Jurisdiction
United States (Federal)
Status
Proposed federal legislation
Dates
H.R.8295 was introduced in the House on April 15, 2026 and referred to the House Committee on the Judiciary. It has not been enacted.
Latest update
April 15, 2026
Update date
2026-04-15

What it does: H.R.8295 would create a federal criminal offense addressing certain abuses involving assisted reproductive technology, including knowingly misrepresenting the nature or source of DNA used in covered reproductive procedures.

Potential impact: The proposal addresses a real-world version of one issue raised by "Wired Womb": using genetic material in reproduction in a manner the affected person did not authorize or understand. Its reach is considerably narrower than the fictional scenario. The legislation addresses misconduct involving assisted reproductive technology; it does not establish rules for an autonomous AI or robot independently making reproductive decisions or operating an artificial gestation system. Questions involving parental status and custody would also generally implicate state family law rather than the federal policy examined for this episode.

  • Congress.gov — H.R.8295, Protecting Families from Fertility Fraud Act of 2026 — Congress.gov

Existing Policy

Existing Policy Existing federal law
Identifier
Public Law 110-233
Jurisdiction
United States (Federal)
Status
Existing federal law
Dates
GINA enacted May 21, 2008 (Public Law 110-233)
Latest update
May 21, 2008
Update date
2008-05-21

What it does: The Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act (GINA) prohibits certain uses of genetic information in health insurance and employment. GINA therefore provides important protections against genetic discrimination, but it is not a comprehensive genetic-data privacy law governing every company that collects or uses DNA information.

Potential impact: For emerging AI systems, the important question is therefore not simply: "Is genetic information protected?" It is also: "Who holds the information, why was it collected, and what is that organization permitted to do with it?" Neither GINA nor HIPAA creates a comprehensive federal framework specifically governing every use of consumer genomic data as an input into AI systems.

Sources

Latest update
Public Law 104-191 (1996)

What it does: The HIPAA Privacy Rule can protect genetic information when that information constitutes protected health information held by a covered entity or applicable business associate. That distinction matters. Genetic information maintained in the health-care system may receive HIPAA protection, while genetic information provided through other consumer or commercial relationships does not automatically receive the same protection merely because the information concerns a person's health or DNA.

Potential impact: For emerging AI systems, the important question is therefore not simply: "Is genetic information protected?" It is also: "Who holds the information, why was it collected, and what is that organization permitted to do with it?" Neither GINA nor HIPAA creates a comprehensive federal framework specifically governing every use of consumer genomic data as an input into AI systems.

Existing Policy Existing federal guidance

Gene editing is no longer purely theoretical. CRISPR/Cas9 technology is already used in FDA-approved gene therapy, while FDA guidance establishes regulatory expectations for investigational human gene-therapy products incorporating genome editing.

Identifier
FDA Guidance
Jurisdiction
United States (Federal)
Status
Existing federal guidance
Dates
April 2026
Latest update
April 2026
Update date
2026-04-01

What it does: FDA's January 2024 guidance addresses development of human gene-therapy products involving genome editing, including product design, manufacturing and testing, nonclinical safety assessment, and clinical-trial considerations. FDA has also issued 2026 draft guidance addressing safety assessment of genome editing using next-generation sequencing.

Potential impact: These frameworks demonstrate that gene editing itself does not exist outside federal oversight. But their focus is primarily on regulated human gene-therapy products and their safety and development—not on a general system of rules governing AI selection or optimization of human genetic characteristics for reproduction. "Wired Womb" therefore raises a different question: how should existing gene-editing safeguards apply if AI becomes increasingly involved in selecting, designing, or directing genetic outcomes?

Sources

  • FDA — Human Gene Therapy Products Incorporating Human Genome Editing, January 2024 — FDA
  • FDA — Safety Assessment of Genome Editing in Human Gene Therapy Products Using Next-Generation Sequencing, Draft Guidance, April 2026 — FDA

Existing Policy Existing federal regulatory framework

Artificial womb technology is also no longer solely a science-fiction concept, although today's research is far removed from the complete artificial gestation depicted in "Wired Womb". FDA has already examined regulatory considerations for artificial womb technology, including clinical-development safeguards and the use of these systems to support extremely premature infants.

Identifier
FDA Medical Device / Clinical Research Framework
Jurisdiction
United States (Federal)
Status
Existing federal regulatory framework
Dates
September 19, 2023
Latest update
September 19, 2023
Update date
2023-09-19

What it does: Existing FDA medical-device and clinical-research frameworks can apply to development and testing of artificial womb technologies intended for medical use. FDA's work in this area has focused on systems intended to support extremely premature infants—not autonomous robotic pregnancy or complete replacement of human gestation.

Potential impact: That distinction is important. Technology designed to help an extremely premature infant continue developing outside the body presents a different set of legal, medical, and ethical questions from technology designed to gestate an embryo or fetus outside a human body from much earlier stages of development. As artificial-womb research progresses, the distinction between neonatal medical support and artificial gestation may become increasingly important for policymakers.

Sources

  • FDA — Background on Artificial Womb Technology — FDA
  • FDA — Pediatric Advisory Committee, Artificial Womb Technology Meeting Materials — FDA

Potential Policy Gap

Potential Policy Gap Partial Gap
Jurisdiction
United States (Federal)
Status
Partial Gap

Potential policy gap: Federal law provides some protection for genetic information, but coverage depends heavily on where the information is held and how it is being used. GINA addresses genetic discrimination in specific contexts. HIPAA protects qualifying health information within its covered framework. The proposed Genomic Data Protection Act would give consumers additional control over genomic data held by covered direct-to-consumer testing companies. But these protections do not create a comprehensive federal framework governing how consumer genetic information may subsequently be analyzed, combined, repurposed, or used by AI systems—including for biological or reproductive purposes. That distinction becomes increasingly important as commercial genetic databases and AI become more capable.

Potential Policy Question

What federal protections, if any, should govern the use of consumer genetic and genomic information by AI systems when that information is analyzed, combined, repurposed, or used for purposes beyond those originally authorized by the consumer?
