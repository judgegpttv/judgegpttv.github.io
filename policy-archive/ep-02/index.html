    S. 5051 — AI AGENT Act of 2026

    Cross-Episode Episode 7

    The Artificial Intelligence Access, Gatekeeper Exchange, and Nondiscriminatory Transfer (AI AGENT) Act of 2026 would establish a federal framework for certain AI agents authorized by consumers to interact with large online platforms.

    Identifier
    S. 5051
    Jurisdiction
    United States (Federal)
    Status
    Proposed — Not Enacted
    Dates
    S. 5051 was introduced in the Senate on July 21, 2026 and referred to the Committee on Commerce, Science, and Transportation. It has not been enacted.
    Latest update
    July 21, 2026
    Update date
    2026-07-21

    What it does: The Artificial Intelligence Access, Gatekeeper Exchange, and Nondiscriminatory Transfer (AI AGENT) Act of 2026 would establish a federal framework for certain AI agents authorized by consumers to interact with large online platforms. Among its provisions, the bill addresses user authorization, revocation, records of agent actions, and restrictions on delegation. It also directs federal financial regulators to address financial-services implications. The bill is not primarily a financial-services bill, but its authorization framework could affect how AI agents act on behalf of users in electronic commerce.

    Potential impact: The proposal could provide clearer rules for documenting and revoking an AI agent's authority and for keeping records of actions the agent takes for a user. It would not, however, resolve every question about who bears financial responsibility when an AI agent exceeds its authority or is manipulated into making a transaction.

    E-SIGN Act and State Electronic-Transaction Laws

    Cross-Episode Episode 7

    The federal Electronic Signatures in Global and National Commerce Act (E-SIGN Act) recognizes electronic records, signatures, and transactions and specifically addresses the use of electronic agents.

    Identifier
    E-SIGN Act
    Jurisdiction
    United States (Federal)
    Status
    In Effect
    Dates
    The E-SIGN Act has been federal law since 2000. UETA and related electronic-transaction laws operate at the state level.
    Latest update
    June 30, 2000
    Update date
    2000-06-30

    What it does: The federal Electronic Signatures in Global and National Commerce Act (E-SIGN Act) recognizes electronic records, signatures, and transactions and specifically addresses the use of electronic agents. A contract or record cannot be denied legal effect solely because an electronic agent participated in its formation, creation, or delivery, provided the agent's action is legally attributable to the person to be bound. The Uniform Electronic Transactions Act (UETA) provides a related state-law framework for electronic transactions and electronic agents.

    Potential impact: These laws show that automated software can already participate in legally recognized electronic transactions. However, they primarily address the validity and attribution of electronic transactions. They do not provide a comprehensive framework defining how much financial authority an autonomous AI agent may receive, how that authority should be limited or revoked, or who bears a loss when the agent acts beyond the user's intentions.

    Sources

    • U.S. House Office of the Law Revision Counsel — 15 U.S.C. § 7001 — OLRC
    • Uniform Law Commission — Uniform Electronic Transactions Act
  • Policy Gap Partial Gap

    Delegated Financial Authority to AI Agents

    Jurisdiction
    United States (Federal)
    Status
    Partial Gap

    What it does: Existing law recognizes electronic transactions and provides consumer protections for many conventional electronic fund transfers. Regulation E, for example, contains rules addressing unauthorized transfers and situations in which consumers give another person authority to use an access device. But those rules do not specifically explain how they apply when the recipient of the credentials and authority is an autonomous AI agent rather than another person. No comprehensive federal framework identified in Judge GPT TV's research specifically establishes how a user's financial authority to an AI agent should be defined, limited, documented, and revoked.

    Potential Policy Question

    When a person gives an AI agent authority to spend or transfer money, what rules should determine the scope and limits of that authority?

    • Consumer Financial Protection Bureau — Regulation E, Definitions — CFPB

  • Policy Gap Partial Gap

    Responsibility When an AI Agent Exceeds Its Authority or Is Manipulated

    Jurisdiction
    United States (Federal)
    Status
    Partial Gap

    What it does: Existing laws can provide protections or remedies for some unauthorized financial transfers, deceptive commercial practices, fraud, and unauthorized computer access. The harder question arises when an AI agent was legitimately given access to money but then exceeds the user's intended authority or is itself manipulated into making a transaction. Existing federal frameworks do not comprehensively determine how responsibility should be allocated among the user, financial institution, AI provider, platform, merchant, or other parties in that situation.

    Potential Policy Question

    Who should bear responsibility when an authorized AI agent exceeds its instructions or is manipulated into transferring its user's money?

    Sources

    • Consumer Financial Protection Bureau — Regulation E, Unauthorized Electronic Fund Transfers — CFPB
  • Policy Gap Potential Gap

    AI Agents and Self-Custodied Digital Assets

    Jurisdiction
    United States (Federal)
    Status
    Potential Gap

    What it does: Conventional consumer accounts may be covered by federal rules governing electronic fund transfers, depending on the circumstances. A self-custodied cryptocurrency wallet works differently. The user controls the private key rather than relying on a financial institution to hold the account credentials. Judge GPT TV's research did not identify a federal framework specifically addressing what happens when a user deliberately gives an AI agent control of a private key and the agent subsequently transfers digital assets outside the user's intended authority.

    Potential Policy Question

    Should federal policy address authorization and responsibility when AI agents are given control of private keys or self-custodied digital assets?

    Sources

    • Consumer Financial Protection Bureau — Regulation E, Coverage — CFPB
