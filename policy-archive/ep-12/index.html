    MIND Act of 2025

    Cross-Episode Episode 11

    The MIND Act is a proposed federal bill addressing neural data and neurotechnology.

    Identifier
    S. 2925
    Jurisdiction
    United States (Federal)
    Status
    Proposed federal legislation
    Dates
    Introduced in the U.S. Senate on September 29, 2025 and referred to the Senate Committee on Commerce, Science, and Transportation.
    Latest update
    September 29, 2025
    Update date
    2025-09-29

    What it does: The bill would direct the Federal Trade Commission to study how neural data is collected, used, protected, and regulated, including whether additional federal privacy protections are needed. It also calls for federal guidance governing federal agency procurement and use of neurotechnology. The proposal recognizes that neural and related data may reveal or help infer cognitive, emotional, psychological, or neurological information.

    Potential impact: The MIND Act could help establish a federal foundation for addressing privacy and autonomy as neurotechnology becomes more capable of accessing or interpreting information connected to the human nervous system. However, the proposal primarily requires study and government guidance. It does not itself establish comprehensive individual rights governing access to neural data or prohibit the kinds of involuntary cognitive integration depicted in "Hivemind Hell".

    NO FAKES Act of 2026

    The NO FAKES Act is proposed federal legislation addressing unauthorized AI-generated and other computer-generated digital replicas of an individual's voice or visual likeness.

    Identifier
    S. 4591 / H.R. 8915
    Jurisdiction
    United States (Federal)
    Status
    Proposed federal legislation
    Dates
    S.4591 was introduced in the Senate on May 20, 2026. On June 24, 2026, the Senate Judiciary Committee reported an amended version and the bill was placed on the Senate Legislative Calendar. H.R.8915, the House companion, was introduced on May 20, 2026 and referred to the House Judiciary Committee. The legislation has not been enacted into law.
    Latest update
    June 24, 2026
    Update date
    2026-06-24

    What it does: The proposal would establish a federal right allowing individuals and other qualifying right holders to authorize the use of a person's voice or visual likeness in highly realistic digital replicas. Importantly for "Hivemind Hell", the proposed right would not simply disappear when a person dies. The Senate-reported legislation provides for postmortem rights that can pass to executors, heirs, assignees, or devisees. The proposal also establishes rules addressing unauthorized digital replicas and responsibilities for certain online services, while providing exceptions and safeguards for protected uses such as news, commentary, criticism, scholarship, satire, and parody.

    Potential impact: AI systems can increasingly recreate aspects of a person from recordings, photographs, video, and other digital material. That raises a question central to "Hivemind Hell" in a form that is already relevant today: Who controls a person's digital identity after they die? The NO FAKES Act would address an important part of that question by creating federal protections for voice and visual likeness, including after death. It would not, however, establish comprehensive rules governing everything that may comprise a person's digital legacy—including personal data, writings, communications, intellectual property, AI training uses, or increasingly sophisticated AI representations constructed from those materials.

    Sources

    • GovInfo — Senate-reported text
    • Congress.gov — H.R. 8915

    Human-Subject and Medical Research Consent

    Existing federal law provides protections that may apply to parts of the episode's scenario, although those protections were generally developed for other purposes.

    Identifier
    45 CFR Part 46 · 21 CFR Part 50
    Jurisdiction
    United States (Federal)
    Status
    Existing federal regulation
    Dates
    Revised Common Rule effective January 21, 2019
    Latest update
    January 21, 2019
    Update date
    2019-01-21

    What it does: Federal regulations under 45 CFR Part 46 — Protection of Human Subjects establish informed-consent protections for covered human-subject research. 21 CFR Part 50 — Protection of Human Subjects provides informed-consent requirements for certain FDA-regulated clinical investigations. These frameworks generally require informed consent from a research subject or, where permitted, a legally authorized representative. They establish protections intended to ensure that people understand what they are authorizing before participating in covered research.

    Potential impact: These rules demonstrate that federal law already recognizes informed consent as an important protection when people participate in regulated research or clinical investigations. Their reach is limited, however. They do not establish a general federal consent framework covering every future commercial technology capable of preserving, recreating, integrating, or manipulating aspects of a person's cognition or identity.

    • eCFR — 45 CFR Part 46
    • eCFR — 21 CFR Part 50

    Federal Intellectual-Property Protections

    Several existing federal frameworks could protect intellectual property even when emerging technology is involved.

    Identifier
    Patents · Copyright · Trade Secrets
    Jurisdiction
    United States (Federal)
    Status
    Existing federal law
    Dates
    USPTO revised AI inventorship guidance, November 2025
    Latest update
    November 26, 2025
    Update date
    2025-11-26

    What it does: Several existing federal frameworks could protect intellectual property even when emerging technology is involved. Patents and AI-Assisted Inventions: U.S. patent law continues to require human inventors. Current USPTO guidance states that AI systems may assist human inventors but do not themselves qualify as inventors. This becomes relevant when technology contributes to, modifies, or generates new material from knowledge originally created by a human. Copyright and AI: U.S. copyright protection remains centered on human authorship. AI can be used as a tool without preventing copyright protection for qualifying human-created expression, while material lacking sufficient human authorship may not qualify for copyright protection. Trade Secrets: The Defend Trade Secrets Act, codified in part at 18 U.S.C. §1836, provides a federal civil remedy for qualifying trade-secret misappropriation. Federal law also provides criminal protections concerning certain theft and misuse of trade secrets, including under the Economic Espionage Act. These protections can apply to qualifying confidential information regardless of whether emerging technology was involved in obtaining it.

    Potential impact: Together, these laws provide meaningful protections for patents, copyrighted works, and qualifying trade secrets. What they do not clearly resolve is what happens when an AI or neurotechnology platform gains access to a person's thoughts, knowledge, unfinished ideas, creative material, or proprietary information and then analyzes, combines, reproduces, or commercially exploits what it obtains.

    Sources

    • U.S. Copyright Office — Copyright and Artificial Intelligence
    • U.S. Code — 18 U.S.C. §1836
    • U.S. Code — 18 U.S.C. §1831

    Neural Data and Cognitive Autonomy

    Jurisdiction
    United States (Federal)
    Status
    Partial Gap

    What it does: Federal policy is beginning to address neural data, but comprehensive protections remain limited. The proposed MIND Act specifically recognizes emerging issues involving neural data and neurotechnology, while existing FDA and other federal frameworks may apply in particular medical or research settings. However, Judge GPT TV's research did not identify a comprehensive federal framework specifically governing private-sector access to and use of cognitive information across medical and nonmedical neurotechnology.

    Consent Beyond Research and Clinical Settings

    Jurisdiction
    United States (Federal)
    Status
    Partial Gap

    What it does: Federal regulations provide established consent protections in covered human-subject research and FDA-regulated clinical investigations. Emerging commercial technologies may not always fall within those frameworks. As technologies capable of interacting with neural activity, digital identity, or human cognition move beyond traditional research and medical environments, questions remain about what consent should be required when technology materially changes what happens to a person or their information.

    Cognitive Information and Intellectual Property

    Jurisdiction
    United States (Federal)
    Status
    Partial Gap

    What it does: Existing patent, copyright, and trade-secret law can protect many forms of human intellectual property. However, these laws were not specifically designed for a world in which neurotechnology or AI might directly access cognitive information and then analyze, combine, reproduce, or commercially exploit what it obtains. Questions may therefore arise over ownership and control when human knowledge or creativity becomes an input into an AI or neurotechnology system.
