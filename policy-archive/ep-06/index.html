    Clean Water Act / NPDES and RCRA

    The Clean Water Act, including the National Pollutant Discharge Elimination System (NPDES), regulates certain discharges of pollutants into waters of the United States. The Resource Conservation and Recovery Act (RCRA) establishes a federal framework for managing hazardous and non-hazardous solid waste.

    Identifier
    Clean Water Act / NPDES · RCRA
    Jurisdiction
    United States (Federal)
    Status
    Existing federal law
    Dates
    Clean Water Act — Public Law 92-500, October 18, 1972. Resource Conservation and Recovery Act — Public Law 94-580, October 21, 1976. Both remain in effect.
    Latest update
    Clean Water Act — October 18, 1972 RCRA — October 21, 1976
    Update date
    1976-10-21

    What it does: The Clean Water Act, including the National Pollutant Discharge Elimination System (NPDES), regulates certain discharges of pollutants into waters of the United States. The Resource Conservation and Recovery Act (RCRA) establishes a federal framework for managing hazardous and non-hazardous solid waste. Federal environmental policy also addresses treatment, recycling, reuse, and resource recovery in particular contexts.

    Potential impact: These frameworks can apply to environmental impacts associated with large temporary gatherings, including wastewater and waste management. But emerging sanitation technology can change the traditional model. Instead of simply collecting waste for treatment or disposal elsewhere, advanced systems may treat wastewater, recycle water, or recover usable resources on-site. That raises the question of how existing environmental frameworks should accommodate new treatment and resource-recovery technologies when they are deployed temporarily and at large scale.

    Noise Control Act

    The Noise Control Act of 1972 established a federal framework for addressing noise that can endanger public health or welfare.

    Identifier
    Noise Control Act of 1972
    Jurisdiction
    United States (Federal)
    Status
    Existing federal law
    Dates
    Enacted October 27, 1972 (Public Law 92-574). Remains in effect; EPA's Office of Noise Abatement and Control funding ended in 1982.
    Latest update
    October 27, 1972
    Update date
    1972-10-27

    What it does: The Noise Control Act of 1972 established a federal framework for addressing noise that can endanger public health or welfare. EPA previously operated a federal noise-control program through its Office of Noise Abatement and Control. Federal funding for that program ended in the early 1980s, although the Noise Control Act remains in effect.

    Potential impact: Large temporary gatherings can use increasingly powerful sound systems and entertainment technologies. Some of those technologies may generate not only conventional audible noise, but also low-frequency sound, vibration, resonance, or other physical effects that raise different measurement questions. Existing federal noise law therefore provides a policy foundation, but emerging sound technologies raise the question of whether conventional approaches adequately address newer types of sonic impact.

    Federal Environmental Monitoring Frameworks

    Federal environmental programs use established testing, monitoring, reporting, and quality-assurance requirements to measure environmental conditions and determine compliance.

    Identifier
    40 CFR Parts 122 · 136 · 53 · 58
    Jurisdiction
    United States (Federal)
    Status
    Existing federal regulations
    Dates
    40 CFR Parts 122, 136, 53, and 58 remain in effect within their respective federal environmental programs.

    What it does: Federal environmental programs use established testing, monitoring, reporting, and quality-assurance requirements to measure environmental conditions and determine compliance. For example, NPDES regulations establish monitoring and reporting requirements for covered water discharges, while federal air-quality programs establish approved monitoring methods and quality-assurance requirements.

    Potential impact: At large temporary gatherings, emerging sensors, real-time monitoring systems, remote sensing, and other technologies could provide environmental information more quickly or measure effects that conventional systems were not designed to capture. Existing federal programs establish reliable monitoring methods within particular regulatory systems. The emerging question is how new forms of environmental measurement should be evaluated and incorporated when they produce reliable information outside those established systems.

    National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA)

    The National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA) establishes a process for federal agencies to consider environmental effects when making certain federal decisions.

    Identifier
    NEPA · Public Law 91-190
    Jurisdiction
    United States (Federal)
    Status
    Existing federal law
    Dates
    Public Law 91-190; Statutes at Large date January 1, 1970 (83 Stat. 852). Remains in effect.
    Latest update
    January 1, 1970
    Update date
    1970-01-01

    What it does: The National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA) establishes a process for federal agencies to consider environmental effects when making certain federal decisions. NEPA can require consideration of environmental impacts and alternatives when applicable to a federal action.

    Potential impact: Large temporary gatherings on or involving federal lands or federal decisions can raise environmental-review questions. Emerging technology may change that analysis in two directions: new treatment or recycling technology may reduce an environmental impact, while new monitoring technology may reveal a risk that was previously difficult to identify. NEPA provides a mechanism for environmental information to inform certain federal decisions, but it does not itself create a comprehensive system for automatically updating environmental requirements whenever technology changes the underlying risk.
