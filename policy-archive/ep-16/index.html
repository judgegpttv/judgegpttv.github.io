    Artificial Intelligence Education and Workforce Readiness Act of 2026

    Identifier
    H.R. 10515
    Jurisdiction
    United States (Federal)
    Status
    Proposed federal legislation
    Dates
    Introduced in the U.S. House of Representatives on September 21, 2026, and referred to the Committee on Education and Workforce and, in addition, the Committees on Science, Space, and Technology and Ways and Means. As of November 14, 2026 (Congress.gov), no further action has been recorded. Not enacted.
    Latest update
    September 21, 2026
    Update date
    2026-09-21

    What it does: This broad proposed legislation addresses AI and workforce readiness. Several of its provisions address AI adoption in education, including educator training, human oversight, student privacy, and responsible procurement. Among its education-related provisions, the bill would add AI-related in-service training to the permitted uses of funds under Title II of the Elementary and Secondary Education Act, supporting the use of AI to improve instruction without replacing educators, while retaining human oversight and decision-making authority. It would also direct the development of an educational AI risk-assessment framework addressing matters such as privacy, bias, safety, surveillance, and procedures for human oversight and override, alongside guidance and model contract clauses for procuring AI tools for education.

    Potential impact: Relevance to the Episode: The bill addresses the distinction between using AI to assist teachers and using it to replace them. Its proposed procurement and risk-assessment measures also relate to the responsibilities of institutions selecting AI instructional systems. Limitation: The provisions concerning educator involvement primarily address training and guidance; they should not be interpreted as a universal legal prohibition on autonomous AI instruction.

    Sources

    • Congress.gov: H.R. 10515, Artificial Intelligence Education and Workforce Readiness Act of 2026
  • Proposed Policy Proposed federal legislation

    Learning Innovation and Family Empowerment with AI Act (LIFE with AI Act)

    Identifier
    S. 3063
    Jurisdiction
    United States (Federal)
    Status
    Proposed federal legislation
    Dates
    Introduced in the U.S. Senate on October 28, 2025, and referred to the Committee on Health, Education, Labor, and Pensions. As of November 14, 2026 (Congress.gov), no further action has been recorded. Not enacted.
    Latest update
    October 28, 2025
    Update date
    2025-10-28

    What it does: This proposed legislation includes provisions on student privacy, parental notification and consent, and transparency and student-data protections in educational technology contracts. As a condition of certain federal education funds, it would require schools to make the privacy policies of educational technology contracts involving student records publicly available at least two weeks before signing, and to require vendors to certify compliance with student-data privacy laws. It would also allow the Department of Education to investigate complaints and place noncompliant vendors on a public list for five years, and it would direct the Department to publish a model student-data privacy agreement. Trade secrets and proprietary information would not have to be made public.

    Potential impact: Relevance to the Episode: The bill relates to the transparency of educational technology contracts and the responsibilities of vendors handling student information, including systems that collect data through individualized instruction. Limitation: Its primary focus is student privacy and contractual transparency. It does not directly establish requirements for disclosing competing instructional alternatives or resolving financial conflicts involving educational consultants.

    Sources

    • Congress.gov: S. 3063, Learning Innovation and Family Empowerment with AI Act
  • Existing Policy Executive direction and guidance

    Federal Direction on AI in Education

    Identifier
    Executive Order 14277 · 91 FR 18774
    Jurisdiction
    United States (Federal)
    Status
    Executive direction and guidance
    Dates
    Executive Order 14277 was signed on April 23, 2025. The Department of Education's Dear Colleague letter is dated July 22, 2025. The Department's final supplemental priority was published on April 13, 2026 and took effect on May 13, 2026.
    Latest update
    May 13, 2026
    Update date
    2026-05-13

    What it does: Federal policy increasingly recognizes AI's potential to support instruction, personalize learning, and improve educational administration. Executive Order 14277, Advancing Artificial Intelligence Education for American Youth (signed April 23, 2025; 90 FR 17519), establishes a federal policy to promote AI literacy and proficiency, including the appropriate integration of AI into education and AI training for educators, and creates a White House Task Force on Artificial Intelligence Education to coordinate federal efforts. The U.S. Department of Education's July 22, 2025 Dear Colleague letter, a nonbinding guidance document, identified permissible uses of federal grant funds for AI-supported educational activities and stated principles for responsible use, including that AI should support educators without replacing the critical role they play, and that AI tools should be accessible, transparent, and protective of student data. On April 13, 2026, the Department of Education also published a final supplemental priority, Advancing Artificial Intelligence in Education (91 FR 18774, effective May 13, 2026), which the Secretary may use in applicable discretionary grant competitions to favor projects involving AI literacy and the appropriate use of AI in education.

    Potential impact: Relevance to the Episode: These initiatives illustrate a federal approach that encourages educational AI while recognizing the importance of human educators. They provide context for the contrasting instructional models depicted in El Steamador and Pistonbury. Limitation: Executive direction, nonbinding guidance, and discretionary-grant priorities do not, by themselves, establish a general requirement that schools retain human teachers when adopting AI instruction.

    Sources

    • Federal Register: Executive Order 14277, Advancing Artificial Intelligence Education for American Youth
    • U.S. Department of Education: Dear Colleague Letter, Guidance on the Use of Federal Grant Funds to Improve Education Outcomes Using Artificial Intelligence (July 22, 2025)
    • Federal Register: Final Priority and Definitions, Secretary's Supplemental Priority on Advancing Artificial Intelligence in Education (91 FR 18774)
  • Existing Policy Existing federal regulation

    Federal Grant Procurement and Conflict-of-Interest Standards

    Identifier
    2 CFR 200.318 to 200.324 · 2 CFR 3474.1
    Jurisdiction
    United States (Federal)
    Status
    Existing federal regulation
    Dates
    The Department of Education adopts 2 CFR Part 200 at 2 CFR 3474.1. The cited procurement sections were last amended on October 1, 2024.
    Latest update
    October 1, 2024
    Update date
    2024-10-01

    What it does: Binding federal grant regulations, particularly 2 CFR 200.318 through 200.324, which the Department of Education has adopted at 2 CFR 3474.1, establish requirements concerning procurement competition, conflicts of interest, cost analysis, and financial management when contracts are supported by federal awards. These requirements can restrict participation by conflicted individuals in procurement decisions, exclude contractors that draft specifications from competing for the resulting contract, address organizational conflicts, and establish procedures intended to protect the integrity of federally funded purchasing.

    Potential impact: Relevance to the Episode: The fictional dispute centers on a financially interested consultant who recommended one educational system without ensuring meaningful consideration of a comparably priced alternative. Federal procurement standards provide a real-world framework for examining such concerns. Limitation: These rules apply only to contracts supported by a federal award. Whether particular provisions apply to an outside consultant depends on that person's role and the funding arrangement. The identified regulations do not expressly establish a universal requirement to disclose every comparable instructional alternative.

    Sources

    • eCFR: 2 CFR Part 200, Uniform Administrative Requirements, Cost Principles, and Audit Requirements for Federal Awards
    • eCFR: 2 CFR 3474.1, Adoption of 2 CFR Part 200
  • Existing Policy Existing federal law and judicial precedent

    Additional Federal Educational and Student Protections

    Identifier
    20 U.S.C. 1232a · 7906a · 7801 · 1232g · 1232h; 42 U.S.C. 2000d
    Jurisdiction
    United States (Federal)
    Status
    Existing federal law and judicial precedent
    Dates
    Statutes and regulations in effect. The Department of Education's Title VI regulations were amended to remove disparate-impact provisions effective July 24, 2026.
    Latest update
    July 24, 2026
    Update date
    2026-07-24

    What it does: Several other legal frameworks provide relevant safeguards. Unless noted as judicial precedent, the following are binding federal statutes and regulations. Educational authority and evidence standards: Federal statutes limit federal direction or control over state and local curricula, programs of instruction, and personnel (20 U.S.C. 1232a), and bar federal officials from mandating specific instructional content or curricula, or conditioning Elementary and Secondary Education Act grants on adopting them (20 U.S.C. 7906a). The Every Student Succeeds Act also defines levels of evidence for specified educational programs and interventions (20 U.S.C. 7801(21)), although these standards do not universally govern all instructional methods. Student privacy: The Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act (FERPA) regulates access to and disclosure of protected education records. Its regulations allow a school to share records with a contractor, such as a technology vendor, as a "school official" only if the contractor performs a function the school would otherwise perform with its own employees, is under the school's direct control regarding the use and maintenance of the records, and uses the information only for the purposes of the disclosure (34 CFR 99.31 and 99.33). The Protection of Pupil Rights Amendment (PPRA) provides protections concerning certain surveys, analyses, and evaluations involving sensitive student information. Educational equity: Title VI of the Civil Rights Act prohibits discrimination based on race, color, or national origin in federally assisted programs. Since July 24, 2026, the Department of Education's Title VI regulations no longer include disparate-impact provisions (91 FR 46733). Judicial precedents provide important but distinct constitutional context: Brown v. Board of Education held that racial segregation in public schools violates the Equal Protection Clause, and San Antonio Independent School District v. Rodriguez held that a state school-finance system producing disparities among districts did not violate the Equal Protection Clause and that education is not a fundamental right under the U.S. Constitution.

    Potential impact: Relevance to the Episode: These protections address aspects of educational governance, student information, and equal opportunity. They also illustrate why differences in instructional methods or resources do not automatically establish violations of federal law. Limitation: Their applicability depends on the specific circumstances. Socioeconomic status alone is not a protected classification under Title VI, and federal law does not generally prescribe a single instructional model for all schools.

    • U.S. Code: 20 U.S.C. 1232g, Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act

    Educational Quality, Human Oversight, and Student Data Governance

    Jurisdiction
    United States (Federal)
    Status
    Partial Gap

    What it does: The fundamental purpose of education extends beyond acquiring information or achieving measurable improvements in test performance. It includes developing understanding, curiosity, critical thinking, independent judgment, and the ability to apply knowledge in unfamiliar situations. AI can support these objectives through personalized instruction, adaptive learning, and assistance to educators. It can also be deployed in ways that prioritize repetition, standardized responses, and operational efficiency. The educational consequences depend not simply on the technology itself, but on how it is designed, implemented, evaluated, and supervised. Existing federal education initiatives encourage responsible AI adoption, while established laws provide certain protections concerning federal funding conditions and student privacy. However, Judge GPT TV's research did not identify a generally applicable federal framework for evaluating the broader educational effectiveness of autonomous AI instruction or clearly allocating responsibility for its outcomes. Federal guidance on the subject is voluntary, and federal law limits federal control over instructional methods, so any such evaluation must account for the primary authority of state and local governments. Another consideration is the extensive student information that AI systems may collect and analyze. FERPA limits how a school may share identifiable student records with contractors and how those contractors may use them, but Judge GPT TV's research did not identify a federal provision that specifically addresses a vendor's retention or reuse of de-identified or aggregated student data, derived analytics, or model adaptations trained on student interactions. H.R. 10515 and S. 3063 would address parts of this area, but neither has been enacted.

    Potential Policy Question

    How can educational institutions determine whether AI is genuinely advancing learning, ensure appropriate human responsibility for educational outcomes, and protect student information, using existing safeguards effectively and identifying additional measures only where justified?

  • Policy Gap Partial Gap

    Informed Decision-Making and Accountability in Public Education

    Jurisdiction
    United States (Federal)
    Status
    Partial Gap

    What it does: Public education institutions are entrusted with decisions that affect students' development, communities' educational opportunities, and the use of taxpayer resources. Responsible stewardship therefore requires more than compliance with procurement procedures. It depends on informed judgment, transparency, independent evaluation, and accountability for the consequences of institutional choices. Existing federal grant regulations establish safeguards addressing procurement competition, financial conflicts of interest, cost analysis, and financial management. However, Judge GPT TV's research did not identify a federal provision that expressly requires an outside adviser to disclose a comparably priced alternative alongside the adviser's own financial interest, or that requires a recipient to document its evaluation of alternatives before adopting an adviser's recommendation. Procedural compliance therefore does not necessarily establish that decision-makers have received a complete and balanced assessment of competing educational approaches. These federal rules apply only to the portion of a contract supported by a federal award; purchases funded locally are governed primarily by state procurement and ethics law. Whether an outside adviser counts as an "agent" of the recipient under the federal conflict-of-interest rule is also not settled in the sources reviewed. The selection of an AI instructional system involves several interrelated considerations: its demonstrated educational benefits, the role of human educators, its long-term operating costs, the reliability of supporting evidence, and the financial interests of those recommending it. A lower initial price or an improvement in a particular performance metric may be meaningful without providing a complete picture of educational value. The fictional dispute illustrates the importance of these distinctions. A financially interested adviser, whose interests were disclosed, recommends an autonomous instructional model without ensuring meaningful consideration of a comparably priced teacher-assisted alternative. The underlying concern is not simply whether a procurement rule was violated, but whether the institution had sufficient information to make a responsible decision on behalf of its students and community. A related, subordinate question is whether federal policy addresses assigning human or automated instruction based on a statistical student profile. Title VI prohibits discrimination based on race, color, or national origin, but socioeconomic status is not a protected classification, and Judge GPT TV's research did not identify a federal policy specifically addressing the practice, which rests largely on state and local decisions.

    Potential Policy Question

    How can public education institutions make informed, transparent, and accountable decisions about AI instructional systems, ensuring that educational value, reliable evidence, long-term costs, and potential conflicts of interest are appropriately considered?
